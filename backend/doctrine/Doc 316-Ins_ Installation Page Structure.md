## Page Purpose

Installation pages capture two distinct reader types:
1. **The DIY researcher** — wondering if they can do this themselves
2. **The informed buyer** — wanting to know what proper installation looks like before hiring

Both need the same information: what good installation actually involves, what typically goes wrong, and what to ask before they hire or attempt.

**The Matt Risinger voice is the ideal here.** He does installation content constantly. He knows the difference between a clean install and a hack install. He is not afraid to say "hire a professional for this." He is also not afraid to explain when and how DIY is reasonable.

## Required Structure

### H1
Lead with the decision or the risk, not the process. "What Gutter Guard Installation Actually Involves — and Why Most Homeowners Should Hire It Out" is stronger than "How to Install Gutter Guards."

### H2 1 — What Proper Installation Actually Involves
Describe what a correct gutter guard installation looks like at every step. Be specific:

- Gutter cleaning and inspection before installation
- How the guard meets the shingle edge (roofline alignment, not fascia attachment)
- Fastening method
- Overlaps, end caps, downspout integration
- Final inspection and water test

Write from contractor experience. What does a job site look like when the installation is done right?

### H2 2 — What Most Installers Get Wrong
This is the diagnostic contractor section. Name the 3–5 most common installation errors:

1. Fascia-only attachment without roofline contact
2. Pitch mismatch — guard installed flat on a steep roof
3. Skipped gutter cleaning before installation
4. Incorrect overlap at seams
5. No downspout integration

For each error: what it looks like, what it causes in year one or two, and how to spot it before you agree to the job.

### H2 3 — DIY vs. Professional: The Real Calculation
Give an honest assessment. What does DIY require in terms of:

- Equipment (ladder height, safety gear)
- Roof condition and pitch
- Experience level
- Warranty implications

When is DIY reasonable? When is it not? Matt Risinger gives real answers, not "always hire a professional" corporate deflection.

**Safety note:** Include the ladder risk disclosure here with the CDC/NIOSH citation.[¹²] Not as a scare tactic — as a genuine risk assessment.

### H2 4 — What to Ask Before You Hire
5–7 specific questions for any installer:

1. Do you install at the roofline or at the fascia?
2. What is the guard's pitch after installation — does it match my roof angle?
3. Do you clean the gutters before installing?
4. What is included in the warranty, and who backs it?
5. What happens to my shingle warranty?
6. Do you have manufacturer installation approval letters?

The questions should be ones a correctly trained installer answers confidently and a corner-cutter fumbles.

### H2 5 — What Good Installation Looks Like After the Fact
Give the homeowner 3–4 things to check after installation is complete:

- Does the guard follow the roofline pitch visually?
- Does water flow across the surface during rain (not drip straight through)?
- Are the seams and end caps sealed?
- Is the first-season performance what was described?

## Callout Guidance (Doc 194)

Doc 194 (Callout Component Library) is the single source for the eight named callouts every writer draws from: **Ask This** (dark navy; the page's "take this to the estimate" moment; universal on every page except pure utility pages, Doc 142 §11B), **How This Benefits You** (green; translates a mechanism into homeowner payoff), **From the Field** (gold; a real Karen or Aaron quote/observation), **The Short Version** (blue; a one-line takeaway ending a dense section), **CTA / action box** (orange; lead → button → trust bar), **Compare This** (purple; a comparison matrix — type vs. what it solves/exposes/performs/what to ask), **Watch For This** (amber; an early-warning checklist of self-checkable signs), and **In Their Words** (teal; a named, attributed customer testimonial from the approved bank in Doc 113 §3.4). **Mix requirement:** every page draws from at least three distinct components — never one type carrying the whole page. Full definitions, placement rules, and style tiers live in Doc 194; do not restate them here.

Installation pages suit **How This Benefits You** (translating install-quality mechanics into homeowner payoff) and **Watch For This** (H2 5, self-checkable signs of a bad install after the fact); **From the Field** fits an installer's-eye observation.

## Voice Rules for Installation Pages

**Do:** Give specific technical descriptions of what correct installation looks like. Homeowners who are about to spend $2,000+ want to know what they are paying for.
**Do not:** Be vague about installation quality. "Properly installed" without specifics is useless.

**Do:** Acknowledge that DIY is possible in some cases without endorsing reckless ladder work.
**Do not:** Lead with "never do this yourself" — that reads as liability management, not genuine advice.

**Do:** Name the ShingleSafe guardian where relevant — this is where ShingleSafe's role in protecting the roof edge during installation is most natural.

## ShingleSafe Connection

Installation pages are the natural home for ShingleSafe explanations. The guardian addresses exactly what installation pages cover: what happens to the shingle edge when a guard is installed, and whether the fastening method preserves or damages the roof.

Include a brief ShingleSafe mention in H2 2 (what most installers get wrong — fascia-only attachment) and a fuller explanation in H2 4 (what to ask — whether the installer has manufacturer approval letters).
