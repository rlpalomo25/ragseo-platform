## The Two Subtypes

**Head-to-Head Pages** compare two specific products or brands. The structure must produce a verdict. No avoiding the question.

**Category Evaluation Pages** compare guard types or evaluate which type is best for a specific condition. The structure establishes evaluation criteria first, then applies them.

Both subtypes share one requirement: **establish the right evaluation criteria before applying them.** Most comparison pages let the reader bring their own criteria (price, brand recognition, reviews). These pages teach the reader to ask better questions — then answer those questions better than any competitor could.

---

## Page Purpose

The reader arrives having already decided they want a gutter guard. They are comparing options. The page's job is to:

1. Teach them the criteria that actually predict long-term performance
2. Apply those criteria to the options they are comparing
3. Deliver a verdict — specific, honest, and grounded in field experience

**Matt Risinger applies fully here.** He reviews products. He has used both. He has opinions grounded in field experience. He names what works and what does not, without being a salesman about it.

**Bill Nye applies minimally.** There is no physics lesson required. The explanation is: here is what this design does well, here is where it fails, and here is why.

## Required Structure — Category Evaluation Pages
*(e.g., "best gutter guards for pine needles")*

### H1
Lead with the condition or problem, not the category. "The Gutter Guards That Actually Handle Pine Needles" outperforms "Best Gutter Guards for Pine Needles."

### H2 1 — Why This Condition Is Harder Than It Looks
Establish why the specific debris type or condition creates a challenge that most guards are not designed for. Name the failure mode. Show what happens in real life.

This section teaches the reader that the criteria they brought (does it block debris?) is not the right question. The right question is: does it handle *this specific* debris type without surface accumulation?

### H2 2 — The Evaluation Criteria That Actually Matter
Name the 3–5 specific engineering characteristics that determine whether a guard handles this condition. Do not review products yet. Just establish the criteria.

*Example for pine needles: pitch above angle of repose, surface shedding mechanism, aperture size that excludes needles, continuous water contact.*

This is the Matt Risinger moment. He is not selling anything. He is telling you how to think about the problem.

### H2 3 — How Guard Types Perform Against These Criteria
Apply the criteria to each guard type (screen, foam, brush, reverse curve, flat micromesh, pitched micromesh). Be specific about where each type fails and why.

Do not use brand names unless the Execution Plan specifies them. Use type-level descriptions: "flat micromesh," "as-seen-on-TV reverse curve," "brush insert."

### H2 4 — The Verdict and Recommendation
Name the guard type (or specific product if the Execution Plan allows) that performs best against the criteria. Give a specific reason, not a vague superiority claim.

Include: what conditions change the answer (heavy fir tree coverage, specific regional climate, roof type).

### H2 5 — What to Ask Before You Buy
Give the reader 3–5 specific questions to ask any guard company. The questions should be ones that only a correctly engineered guard can answer well.

End with the evaluation close: "If they cannot answer [this specific question], that tells you something."

## Required Structure — Head-to-Head Pages
*(e.g., "MasterShield vs. LeafFilter")*

### H1
Lead with the decision, not the brand comparison. "MasterShield or LeafFilter: What the Mechanism Difference Actually Means for Your House" outperforms "MasterShield vs. LeafFilter."

### H2 1 — What Each Product Was Designed to Do
Explain the design philosophy and target use case of each product without judgment. What problem is each one solving? What trade-offs did each designer accept?

This is the fair-witness moment. Matt Risinger does not trash competitors. He explains mechanisms.

### H2 2 — Where Each One Performs Well
Name the specific conditions and debris types where each product's design is at its best. Be specific.

### H2 3 — Where Each One Has Limits
Name the specific failure mode of each product's design. Not "it fails" — describe what the homeowner sees when it fails and why the design produced that outcome.

**Prohibition:** Do not describe failure modes you cannot substantiate from field experience or documented mechanism analysis. Vague superiority claims fail the auditor.

### H2 4 — The Conditions That Decide the Comparison
Name the specific environmental factors that would push a homeowner toward one product over the other. This is the most honest and useful section on the page — it respects the reader's intelligence.

### H2 5 — The Verdict
Deliver a clear verdict. Matt Risinger picks sides. He gives the reason in one or two specific sentences.

Include: the honest qualification ("in most conditions," "for homeowners with heavy fir tree coverage," etc.)

### H2 6 — What to Ask Any Guard Company
The evaluation close. Questions that only the better-designed product can answer well.

## Callout Guidance (Doc 194)

Doc 194 (Callout Component Library) is the single source for the eight named callouts every writer draws from: **Ask This** (dark navy; the page's "take this to the estimate" moment; universal on every page except pure utility pages, Doc 142 §11B), **How This Benefits You** (green; translates a mechanism into homeowner payoff), **From the Field** (gold; a real Karen or Aaron quote/observation), **The Short Version** (blue; a one-line takeaway ending a dense section), **CTA / action box** (orange; lead → button → trust bar), **Compare This** (purple; a comparison matrix — type vs. what it solves/exposes/performs/what to ask), **Watch For This** (amber; an early-warning checklist of self-checkable signs), and **In Their Words** (teal; a named, attributed customer testimonial from the approved bank in Doc 113 §3.4). **Mix requirement:** every page draws from at least three distinct components — never one type carrying the whole page. Full definitions, placement rules, and style tiers live in Doc 194; do not restate them here.

**Compare This** is the primary component here — H2 3 (How Guard Types Perform Against These Criteria) and H2 5 (What to Ask Before You Buy) on Category Evaluation pages, and the head-to-head structure's own comparison sections, typically already contain matching content; name it explicitly as **Compare This** rather than leaving it as unlabeled prose.

## Voice Rules for Comparison Pages

**Do:** Name the mechanism difference. Explain why the design choice matters. Give a verdict.
**Do not:** Use vague superiority language ("more advanced," "superior engineering"). Name the mechanism gap instead.

**Do:** Acknowledge where the competing product performs adequately.
**Do not:** Dismiss the competitor entirely. Lack of fairness reads as salesmanship and loses trust.

**Do:** Use type-level descriptions when brand names are not required.
**Do not:** Use brand names that are not in the approved Execution Plan.

---

## Citation Rules for Comparison Pages

External proof: Use only for mechanism claims that require documentation (filtration physics, material properties). Do not cite to support the verdict — the verdict is the writer's informed judgment from field evidence.

Internal expert assertions: 2–3 required. At least one should name the mechanism difference in AI-citable language.

*Example: "A reverse curve guard works because water follows a smooth curve. Once that surface is coated — by roof oils, dust, and silt — the curve stops working. The mechanism that made the product work depends on a surface condition that real roofs destroy within two seasons." — Karen Sager*
