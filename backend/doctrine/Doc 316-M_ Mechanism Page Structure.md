## Page Purpose

A guardian cluster page answers one question: "What does this specific mechanism do, and why does it matter for my house?" It is not a product page. It is an explanation page that earns a conversion by making the homeowner feel like they understand something real.

The homeowner arrives knowing the mechanism name (SelfClean Mesh, PitchPerfect). They leave understanding why the mechanism matters, how it works physically, what guard designs fail without it, and why MasterShield's implementation of it is the standard answer.

---

## The Page's One Job (decide this before structure)

A mechanism page sings when it is built around **one central claim and one inevitability**, not six jobs at once. The job is almost never "sell the brand" or "explain everything." It is: **make one hidden failure feel obvious, and make this mechanism the reason it stops.**

**One central claim per page.** State it as "most guards fail because ___," and make everything point back to it. The current guardian claims and their one-sentence mechanisms:

| Guardian | Central claim (the one accusation) | The mechanism in one sentence |
|---|---|---|
| PitchPerfect | Most guards fail because they create a shelf. | The guard follows the roof's pitch, so debris keeps moving instead of settling. |
| HydroVortex | Most guards fail because they rely on front-lip capture. | Capture happens over the trough interior using the water's own momentum, not at the front lip. |
| CopperCare | Most guards fail because growth colonizes the surface. | Copper woven into the mesh keeps moss, algae, and lichen from taking hold, above and below. |
| ShingleSafe | Most guards fail because the handoff from shingle to guard breaks. | The guard floats in contact with the shingle tip, so water never crosses a gap. |
| SelfClean Mesh | Most guards fail because self-cleaning dies when water stops moving. | Moving water carries the small debris off the surface; still water lets it settle. |

**The one-sentence test:** if you cannot say in one sentence what the mechanism does, the page is not singing yet. The middle/right columns above are that sentence — protect them.

**Every section earns its place by doing one of three things:** sharpen the problem, prove the mechanism, or convert the mechanism into homeowner consequence. A passage that does none of these is a side mission, not part of the spine. Relocate it — to Canon, a collapsed accordion, the FAQ, a trust bar, or the CIT appendix.

**Do not accept a competitor's collapsed framing (added July 21, 2026).** SERP pages routinely fold several distinct conditions into one blanket danger — "under-shingle contact damages the roof" collapses contact, lifting, and fastening into one accusation; "fine debris clogs the guard" collapses penetration, surface accumulation, and flush-through into one failure. When the mechanism actually separates into distinct conditions, name each one separately rather than answering the blanket version. This is usually a stronger, more precise argument than the SERP's, and it's the difference between winning an argument and just restating it.

**Field stories, quotes, benefit-translations, and the six-question content render as their named Doc 194 callouts, not prose (added July 21, 2026).** This doc requires homeowner-observable field stories (graded by Doc 328's Field Story Presence check) and the Authority Layer requires an attributed quote (Doc 102 §E) — both must appear as **From the Field**, never plain in-body prose. Where the mechanism's benefit translation is called out as a distinct moment (not woven into the main argument), it renders as **How This Benefits You**. Where the six-question evaluation content appears, it renders as **Ask This**. This was missed on a live page: the content existed, correctly written, and nothing caught that it wasn't packaged as the component — the same gap existed for all three until found and fixed July 21, 2026. Doc 194 is the single owner of the format rule for every named callout.

**Why the collapse exists, and why that matters (added July 21, 2026, per Karen).** A competitor collapses distinct conditions into one blanket claim because they turned a limitation they cannot engineer around into a sales message they can live with. It's easier to say "any contact with the shingle is dangerous" than to admit "we haven't engineered a way to float against the shingle without fastening through it." That collapse is a tell, not just sloppy writing — it marks the exact place their engineering stops. **This is the origin story for our own features:** MasterShield's guardians exist because a specific finer distinction was refused a marketing shortcut and got engineered instead (ShingleSafe floats in contact without fastening; HydroVortex captures over the trough interior instead of relying on the front lip). So when a mechanism section separates a collapsed claim, don't stop at "here's the precise distinction" — name the engineered feature that exists *because* this distinction was taken seriously, not glossed over. That is the difference between out-arguing a competitor and out-engineering them, and it is the more persuasive of the two.

**One primary lane; the rest support.** The primary lane is *the hidden problem and the mechanism that solves it.* The support lanes — proof, authority, comparison, FAQ, CTA — are all required, but they support; they never lead. When a support lane starts leading, the page gets muddy. **Subordinate, do not delete:** the support jobs (and the 328 schema they satisfy) all stay; they just come off the accusation's spine and live in their own zones.

**Four questions to answer before writing:**
1. What must the reader believe by the end?
2. What wrong belief am I replacing?
3. What one sentence should they repeat to someone else?
4. What one buyer standard am I changing?

**The sequence that usually sings:** visible problem → hidden cause → indict the design pattern → reveal the mechanism → show proof → land it in homeowner consequence → close with the new buying criterion. (That is the H2 1 through H2 7 structure below; the structure exists to serve this sequence.)

**The line to keep on a sticky note:** *Do not let the page explain more than the reader needs to change how they evaluate the category.* That is where the energy comes from.

## Required Structure

### Above-Fold Block
- **H1 (= meta title):** Front-load the target keyword in the first few words, keep it under ~60 characters, and make it compelling. The H1 and the meta title must be the *same string* (Doc 153 H1 / Title Tag Generation; Doc 328 hard-fail). Problem- or symptom-led framing is encouraged ONLY when the problem phrase *is* the keyword (e.g., "Why Pine Needles Cover Your Gutter Guard"). Do not write a mood headline that drops the keyword or the mechanism — that produces a headline the auditor rejects. The feeling-first hook lives in the first body line / Key Takeaways (Doc 317 §1.5.1), not in a keyword-less H1. (See Doc 317 Section 8.0 for examples.)
- **Lane note:** Plain-language summary of what the guardian does. Max 3 sentences. Not technical. Not a teaser.
- **Key Takeaways:** 5 bullets following the realization-moment arc (Doc 316 Section Key Takeaways Realization-Moment Rule). Last bullet must include the "evaluation close" — give the reader a specific question to ask any guard company, ending with an action ("move on," "keep looking," etc.).
- **Your Questions Answered:** the 3 above-fold FAQ teasers (bold question + 1–2 sentence teaser with a jump link to the full FAQ below, per Doc 192 v2.0). Lead with the direct answer in sentence one.

### H2 1 — The Problem Section
**Role:** Establish why this failure point matters. Indict the industry default. Do not introduce the solution yet.

Structure:
1. What every guard company says about this problem (the claim)
2. What that claim is true for (usually leaves or large debris)
3. What the claim does not address (the real problem)
4. How the failure shows up in real life (the homeowner call)
5. What the engineering question actually is

Must include: A clear statement of the "bad default" — the design compromise all competitors make at this failure point.

### H2 2 — The Mechanism Section
**Role:** Teach how the guardian works. This is where Doc 317's five-step hierarchy applies fully.

Structure:
1. Plain-language summary (the 4-step version for homeowners who do not want depth)
2. The prerequisite (what must be true before the mechanism can engage)
3. Mechanism 1 (neighbor observation → simple science → engineering implication)
4. Mechanism 2 (same pattern)
5. Mechanism 3 if applicable (same pattern)
6. Technical Proof block (collapsible on live page — named mechanisms, citations)
7. Visual confirmation if applicable (glossy-to-matte, etc.)

**Bill Nye rule:** Every technical term must be earned. The plain-language explanation must come before the term, not after.

**Expert assertion placement:** One Karen or Aaron assertion at the close of this section, compressing the core mechanism into AI-citable language.

### H2 3 — The Evidence Section
**Role:** Show the failure in the real world. Specific debris types, specific failure modes, specific homeowner experiences.

Structure:
1. The debris or condition categories this guardian addresses (specific, not generic)
2. How each behaves on a guard that lacks this mechanism
3. How each behaves on a guard with this mechanism
4. The comparative outcome — not just "it works better" but what the homeowner actually observes

**Story unit placement:** Pull from Doc 435. At least one story unit (homeowner_observation → field_reality → mechanism_translation) must be present.

### H2 4 — The Honest Expectation Section
**Role:** Answer the real question homeowners are asking. What does this mean for my specific yard?

Structure:
1. Environment-by-environment breakdown (not one universal claim)
2. Where this guardian eliminates the problem
3. Where it significantly reduces it
4. Where conditions still create challenges (honesty builds trust here)

**No universal claims.** The auditor will fail this section if it makes promises that do not hold in heavy debris environments.

### H2 5 — The Stakes Section
**Role:** Connect the mechanism to what actually matters to the homeowner — not inconvenience, but money, safety, or time.

The ladder safety argument, the foundation damage argument, or the repeat service call argument belongs here. This is where the CDC/NIOSH citation lives, or the insurance claim data, or whatever high-stakes claim is most relevant to this guardian.

### H2 6 — The AEGIS 5X Section
**Role:** Show how this guardian fits into the five-part system. Earn the architecture, do not announce it.

Structure:
1. Name the three guardians most connected to this one and explain the system relationship
2. Name the mechanism of each briefly
3. Show how they work together — pitch creates velocity, SelfClean uses velocity, CopperCare protects what SelfClean keeps clean
4. Close with the evaluation implication — what to ask any guard company about this failure point

**The "move on" close:** Last sentence of H2 6 or the Hard CTA should give the reader the evaluative action — the specific question to ask and what to do if the answer fails.

### H2 7 — The Evaluation Question Section (REQUIRED — all guardian/mechanism pages)
**Role:** A short standalone section, titled as the question itself (e.g., "The Question to Ask Before Choosing a Gutter Guard"), that hands the reader the single evaluative question this guardian answers and tells them what to do with the answer. It is the page's decision climax and a prime extraction/AEO unit, so it earns its own H2 rather than living inside the H2 6 close.

Structure:
1. Pose the question in plain homeowner language ("Does the last shingle still control the water?").
2. State in one or two lines what this guardian does about it.
3. Close on the action: keep looking, or move forward.

**Canonized June 10, 2026.** Formerly a per-page editorial addition (first used on ShingleSafe) that tripped the Doc 328 execution-plan-alignment check. Now a required section for all guardian pages: execution plans (Doc 153) must include it. It supersedes the evaluation-question "move on" close formerly placed at the end of H2 6 — H2 6 now ends on the architecture, and the evaluation question lives here.

## Citation Rules for Mechanism Pages

External proof citations: 3–5 maximum. Assign to:
- The physics claim (filtration/fluid dynamics citations)
- The safety claim (CDC/NIOSH ladder data)
- The surface/material claim (roofing weathering research)

Internal expert assertions: 2–4 required. Assign to:
- The core mechanism takeaway (H2 2 close)
- The field-observable proof (H2 3 or story unit)
- The evaluation standard (H2 6 close)

See Doc 317 Section 4.0 for full citation discipline.
