## Page Purpose

The reader is price-researching. They have a number in their head — usually the wrong number for the wrong reason. The page's job is to reframe the cost question from "how much does it cost" to "what am I actually comparing when I compare prices."

Matt Risinger applies fully. He has given hundreds of bids. He knows what cheap means in practice. He does not talk homeowners into spending more than they should. He talks them into understanding what they are actually buying before they choose.

Bill Nye is not needed here. There is no physics to translate. The complexity is economic and practical, not scientific.

---

## The Core Reframe

Most homeowners arrive asking: "What does a gutter guard cost?"

The page should leave them asking: "What does the wrong gutter guard cost over time?"

Price comparison without total cost of ownership is incomplete. The page should teach the reader to add: installation quality, maintenance reduction, failure modes, and the cost of the problem the guard was supposed to prevent.

---

## Front-load and FAQ handling

Two rules from the system-wide FAQ/SOT update apply to every pricing page:

- **Front-load the answer.** Put a clean, specific cost answer (a real range, year-stamped, with the "it depends on the home, here is what moves it" framing) in or just under Key Takeaways — within the first ~30% of the page. Most AI citations are pulled from the opening, so the cost answer cannot wait until H2 5.
- **FAQs are SOT-written and embedded here, not on a separate page.** Cost questions (per foot, by home size, average install) are written by the SOT Agent to its compression standard and embedded on this page; the writer does not rewrite them. The above-the-fold **Your Questions Answered** block renders as the `.qa` component (bold question + a 1-2 sentence teaser + a jump link to the full FAQ answer), **not** flowing prose — Doc 192 v2.0 reversed the earlier de-staged-prose rule. One answer per question: category-level questions ("are gutter guards worth it") live on their owning pillar and are linked to, not duplicated here.

## Required Structure

### H1
Lead with the decision the reader is actually making, not the price question. "What a Gutter Guard Actually Costs: The Number That Matters Is Not the Sticker Price" is stronger than "How Much Do Gutter Guards Cost?"

### H2 1 — The Price Range and What Drives It
Give the real price range across the category. Be specific (per linear foot, full installation on a typical home). Explain the three main variables that move the price:

1. Material and mesh quality
2. Installation method (fascia-only vs. roofline alignment)
3. Labor and warranty

Do not use this section to position MasterShield yet. Just establish what actually determines the number.

### H2 2 — What Low Price Usually Means in Practice
This is the contractor's honest assessment. Name what typically gets compromised at the low end: material quality, installation quality, warranty depth, or the mechanism itself.

Use specific, field-observable failure examples. Not "lower quality" — what the homeowner actually sees two years after a cheap installation.

*Example: "The most common call after a cheap guard installation is not 'it clogged.' It is 'it's pulling away from the roof.' The fastening method on most value guards requires prying or drilling into the fascia — and fascia is not where a gutter guard should be anchored if you want it to follow the roofline."*

### H2 3 — Total Cost of Ownership
Introduce the total cost of ownership framework. What does the homeowner actually spend over 10 years, including:

- Installation cost
- Maintenance calls (or DIY ladder time)
- Any damage from a guard that failed at its job
- Replacement costs if the product needs replacing

This is where the math works in favor of a premium product without having to say "premium product." The argument is arithmetic, not salesmanship.

### H2 4 — What You Are Actually Comparing
Name the 4–5 specific product and installation characteristics that determine 10-year performance. Translate each into what the homeowner will see at year two or year five.

This is the evaluation criteria section. Give the reader the questions to ask before they sign a contract.

### H2 5 — What MasterShield Costs and What That Gets You
Only after the value framework is established, introduce the MasterShield price point and what it includes. Do not lead with price. Lead with what the price reflects.

If pricing guidelines from Doc 316 apply, use them here.

### H2 6 — What to Ask Before You Sign
3–5 specific questions for any guard company. The questions should be ones where a premium installation answers clearly and a commodity installation fumbles.

End with the evaluation close.

## Voice Rules for Pricing Pages

**Do:** Use specific numbers. Vague price ranges ("varies widely") are useless.
**Do not:** Use pricing that conflicts with Doc 316 Section 4 guidelines.

**Do:** Name what cheap installations fail at, specifically.
**Do not:** Trash competitors by name unless the Execution Plan authorizes it.

**Do:** Give the total cost of ownership argument in concrete terms.
**Do not:** Exaggerate failure rates or repair costs beyond what field experience supports.

---

## Citation Rules for Pricing Pages

External proof: Use for insurance claim statistics (water damage costs, foundation repair costs) if those support the total cost of ownership argument. Doc 113 Section 4.1 (Insurance Information Institute data) is relevant here.

Internal expert assertions: 2–3 required. At least one should give a field-grounded perspective on what cheap installation looks like.

*Example: "The price of a gutter guard is not what you pay the day it goes on. It is what you pay the day you find out it was not doing what it was supposed to do." — Aaron Kapfer*
