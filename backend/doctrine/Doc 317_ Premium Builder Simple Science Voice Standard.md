## 1.0 The Voice Model

**The standard:** Write like Matt Risinger — the premium builder who has seen everything, knows what works, and will tell you exactly what he would put on his own house. He is not a salesman. He is the trusted expert neighbor a homeowner wishes they had. He names failure modes. He gives verdicts. He respects the reader's intelligence.

When the topic requires explaining a physical mechanism — how water moves, why debris sticks, what happens at a filter opening — Matt does not become someone else. He reaches for the Bill Nye approach to the science: the same authority, now teaching.

**The Bill Nye approach — four things it does:**

1. **Holds a reading level everyone can follow.** The explanation lands for a curious teenager and a busy homeowner alike. It never goes over anyone's head and never requires prior knowledge. If a sentence assumes the reader already understands engineering, it has failed.
2. **Leads with analogies.** Every mechanism is explained first through something the reader already knows — swinging a bucket, water running off a windshield, a sieve versus a funnel. The everyday image comes before the technical term, always.
3. **Makes the science feel fun and engaging.** The reader should feel the small click of "oh — that's actually clever," not the dread of a textbook. Curiosity, not obligation. The science is the interesting part of the page, not the part to endure.
4. **Avoids the goofy.** This is Bill Nye's clarity and energy, not his sound effects and costumes. No gimmicks, no jokes that undercut the authority, no talking down. Engaging because it's clear and genuinely interesting — never because it's silly.

The test: a reader finishes a mechanism section and thinks *"I understand exactly why that works, and that was kind of interesting"* — without ever feeling lectured, confused, or entertained at the expense of substance.

**This standard applies to all B2C pages** — MasterShield and Klean Gutter — regardless of page type.

### Words we never use in page copy

The brand does not perform virtues or make absolute promises. Never write, in published copy:

- **"honest" / "honesty"** — claiming honesty performs it instead of demonstrating it; earn trust by being specific. **This ban covers the execution plan's own prose too, not only page copy** — do not write "the honest answer," "three honest buckets," or "honest attribution"; use "the direct/real answer," "straight talk," "plainly." (`honesty_constraints` stays a valid *plan field name*; the word must never appear as a descriptor anywhere a human reads it — page or plan.)
- **"never clean your gutters again"** and every variant — a competitor's slogan and a promise we cannot keep. Not even as a quoted bad example. Use **"virtually eliminates"** instead.
- **"best in the world," "guaranteed," "the only," "most advanced,"** or any absolute promise.

Auditor enforcement: Doc 328 Prohibited Phrases. Write it right here so the auditor never has to catch it.

**B2B pages (MicroMeshGutterGuards.com)** use the same Matt Risinger authority but in a business-partner register. The trusted expert who knows the product deeply, speaking to a contractor who also knows the industry. The Bill Nye explanation style is not needed — the audience is trade, not homeowner.

> "Here's what's actually happening on the roof. You don't need a fluid dynamics degree to see it. Water moving across a pitched surface carries debris. Water falling straight down leaves debris behind. That one difference explains why some guards keep working and others slowly turn into the clog."

That is the target register.

## 1.5 The Pull — Forward Momentum (the quality that makes a page sing)

The single thing that separates writing that *sings* from writing that is merely correct is **momentum**: every sentence makes the reader want the next one. The reader is carried, not taught. This is the quality the AEGIS 5X pillar page got right first — the page the whole team reacted to with "give us more" — and it is the target for every page. It is built from seven repeatable moves. Use them deliberately; they are not decoration.

1. **The two-beat turn.** A statement, then a short reframe. *"That's the promise. It's also the limit."* / *"That's not a debris problem. That's a water problem."* / *"The system doesn't change. The rain does."* It is the rhythm of confident speech, and it lands a small hit of momentum every time.
2. **One idea per paragraph, and keep paragraphs short.** Ideas arrive single file. Never make the reader hold five clauses at once. The white space is part of the easy listening. (Pairs with the 4-sentence rule in Section 6.0.)
3. **Feeling first, engineering second.** Open a section on what the homeowner sees, fears, or feels, then let the mechanism serve the feeling. *"You know the feeling. It's raining hard. It's late."* The engineering is in the room; it is never the host.
4. **Talk to the reader, in the present tense.** Second person, direct address, the occasional imperative. *"Don't ask 'does it work?' Ask how it works."* It is a conversation, not a spec sheet.
5. **Make music with parallelism.** Triads and matched fragments. *"Five failure points. Five engineered responses. One system."* / *"No prying. No pressure. No damage."* The ear remembers them.
6. **Calm certainty, plain words.** No hedging, no jargon stacks. The knowledgeable neighbor who has seen it all and isn't impressed by marketing. State the verdict; don't qualify it to death.
7. **Feature, then its benefit — never one without the other (added August 5, 2026, Karen).** Every claim pairs the two in the same breath: what it is, then what that actually means for the reader. *"CopperCare weaves copper into the mesh [feature], so what's collecting under it never gets the chance to grow into a bigger problem [benefit]."* A feature alone reads like a spec sheet. A benefit alone reads like a promise with nothing behind it. Paired, the reader's internal voice says *yes* — and a page is a string of those, not one big pitch at the end. This is the mechanical "how" underneath Schwartz's "mechanism to earn belief" and Tony Robbins's "not just the feature" (Doc 100 §11.0) — those rows say what to protect; this move is how a single sentence actually does it.

**What breaks the Pull (stop-and-study prose):** long sentences with stacked clauses; a section that opens with a mechanism or a definition instead of a feeling; three technical paragraphs in a row with no turn, no scene, no parallel; hedged, over-qualified verdicts. When a stretch of a page makes the reader concentrate instead of coast, the Pull is gone, even if every fact is right.

**The test:** read a section aloud. If you naturally lean in for the next sentence, the Pull is working. If you have to slow down and re-read to hold the meaning, rewrite for momentum: shorten, turn, scene, parallel.

**Page-type note:** mechanism and cluster pages carry inherent density — their job is one mechanism in depth. The Pull does not mean dumbing the mechanism down. It means layering these seven moves over the technical content so the *main line* stays easy-listening, while the deepest detail lives in the optional/accordion lane (Section 2.0, Step 5). The pillar earns the read; the cluster page rewards it. Both should pull.

*Enforcement: the Writer (Doc 316) opens each section with a felt frame before the mechanism and applies the seven moves; the Auditor (Doc 328) checks for the Pull (feeling-first openings, the two-beat turn, single-idea paragraphs, feature-benefit pairing) as a quality flag.*

### 1.5.1 The Top of Every Page Is the Skimmer's Page

Every page opens with a Key Takeaways block (labeled `## Key Takeaways` — never `Summary` or `TL;DR`): the block that serves the reader who will **not** read the whole page. It is the most-read real estate on the page, and it does three jobs at once:

1. **Pull them in** — the Pull's feeling-first opening, not a definition or a mechanism.
2. **Answer a couple of the highest-intent questions plainly** — so a skimmer leaves informed, and the above-fold answers double as AEO extraction blocks.
3. **Give them somewhere to act** — a reader who is satisfied at the top is a reader ready to convert; capture that intent here, do not make them scroll for it.

**Format is flexible.** Bullets are fine but not required; a short felt lead-in followed by two or three plain answers works just as well. What is not flexible is the intent: lead with the Pull, answer the questions a ready buyer would ask, and never bury the payoff behind the engineering. (Schema and quality-gate detail: Doc 153 Key Takeaways Generation + above-fold FAQ, Doc 155 Section 5.)

**Where the headline fits.** The H1 is not where the Pull's full emotional hook lives. The H1 must equal the page's meta title and lead with the target keyword (Doc 153 H1 / Title Tag Generation; Doc 328 hard-fail), so it carries the keyword in a tight, compelling promise. The feeling-first opening lives in the first body line and the Key Takeaways block beneath it. A keyword-front H1 and a feeling-first body opening are a relay, not a competition: do not drop the keyword to make the H1 emotional, and do not let the H1's keyword discipline drain the Pull from the lines below it. A keyword-correct H1 that is boring still fails (Doc 328 H1 Hook Quality).

**Do not flatten the openings.** Section openings on guardian pages lead with the Pull — a neighbor observation, a field story, the lived moment — not a definition or a direct answer. The answer-first extraction load is carried by the above-fold Your Questions Answered and the Extractable Blocks (CIT) appendix, never by the section openings. Do not strip a story opening into a generic answer block to satisfy a checklist: doing so removes the page's differentiation and fails Doc 328's Q4/Q5 (and the Doc 328 Guardian-Page answer-first exception exists precisely so you never have to). Compliance lives in the Your Questions Answered and CIT blocks; the voice lives in the openings.

## 1.6 No Meta-Narration — The Voice Never Explains Itself (added August 1, 2026, Karen)

A neighbor talking to a neighbor never describes the conversation he is having. He does not say "in this section," "as covered above," "what this means for you is," or "the next part explains." He tells you what usually goes wrong, what he's seen fail, and what to check for. **The voice never refers to itself, its own structure, or its own reasoning — only to the homeowner's problem.**

**Banned constructions (the shape is banned, not just the exact wording):**

- **Document self-reference:** "this section," "this article," "this page," "this guide," "as discussed above," "as we'll cover below," "in the next part."
- **Reasoning self-reference:** "the point here is," "what we're getting at is," "to explain this," "here's why this matters."
- **Reader-instruction self-reference:** "what comes next," "what you need to understand is," "you'll want to keep this in mind," "keep reading to find out."

**Say it the way the neighbor actually would instead.** Every one of those constructions is standing in for a plain statement about the homeowner's world — trade the meta-commentary for the content it was gesturing at, not for a smoother version of the same gesture:

| Instead of (meta-narration) | Say (the homeowner's world) |
|---|---|
| "This section explains why flat guards fail in heavy rain." | "Flat guards buckle in heavy rain because water has nowhere to go but over the top." |
| "What comes next is the real problem with mesh size." | "Here's the part nobody mentions when they're selling you on mesh size." |
| "As discussed above, inertia matters here." | (Just build on the point already made — don't announce that you're building on it.) |

**This governs forward-looking transitions too (Doc 316/320/324 §4A's "forward hook").** A section may end by naming the *next piece of trouble the homeowner is about to face* — a concrete, in-the-world problem, event, or failure — but never by naming "the next section." "This is calm. The next one is not" describes an upcoming failure point, not a heading; it never becomes "the next section covers what happens after this."

**What this looks like when it's working.** `Craft-Notes-Momentum-Sentences-and-Link-Discipline.md` (the source document behind Doc 316/320/324 §4A's forward-hook rule) captured these from a real page transition that worked:

> "Water you can watch is one kind of problem. The next one grows where you cannot see it." *(closes an overflow section, opens a growth section)*
> "Ice is seasonal. The next tenant moves in year-round." *(closes an ice section, opens a pests section)*

Neither line says "the next section." Both name the next *trouble* — something the homeowner will actually face — in the same breath that closes the trouble just covered. That is the rule in practice: the hook always points at the world, never at the document.

**Enforcement:** Doc 328 Section 2B (paragraph-level hard fail) and Doc 1710 Section 11 (human sanity check).

## 2.0 The Five-Step Teaching Hierarchy

**For mechanism and guardian cluster pages only.** (See page-type variants in Section 5.0 for other page types.)

Every mechanism explanation section must follow this sequence:

**Step 1 — Neighbor Observation**
What does the homeowner actually see, notice, or experience? Start here. This is the field-observable moment that opens the reader to the explanation.

*Example: "The homeowners who notice this first are usually the ones who've had other guards before. They expect to climb up in spring and find a mat of debris. They don't."*

**Step 2 — Simple Science Explanation**
What is actually happening physically? Explain it in plain language before giving it a name. Use an analogy from everyday life. Cause-and-effect only. No jargon until the explanation has earned the term.

*Example: "Think of swinging a bucket of wet sand in a circle and then stopping the bucket suddenly. The sand does not stop when the bucket does — it keeps going, flying straight ahead. That is inertia. The same thing happens at a filter opening."*

**Step 3 — Engineering Implication**
What does this physical reality mean for how a guard should be designed? What does a guard that understands this do differently?

*Example: "A guard designed around this creates the direction change at every filter opening, every time it rains. A guard installed flat never creates the direction change at all."*

**Step 4 — AEGIS 5X Connection**
How does the specific guardian solve this failure point? Name the guardian and its job in the system.

*Example: "This is what SelfClean Mesh is engineered to do. It is not one feature — it is what happens when pitch, water flow, and filter geometry work together."*

**Step 5 — Optional Deeper Proof (collapsible)**
The technical name, the citation, the formal mechanism. Available for readers who want it. Should not interrupt the reading experience for those who do not.

*Example: "The technical term for the first cleaning action is inertial impaction. The second is turbulent flow separation. The citations are in the references section if you want to go deeper."*

**The rule:** Do not lead with Step 5. Do not skip Steps 1 and 2. Technical terms are optional destinations, not mandatory stops.

## 3.0 The Teach-Then-Prove Rule

**Teach the mechanism in plain language first. Name the technical principle second.**

| Wrong order | Right order |
|---|---|
| "Inertial impaction causes particles to dislodge at the aperture." | "When water changes direction at a filter opening, the particles in the water keep going straight — which drives them into the opening wall. That impact dislodges debris. The technical name for this is inertial impaction." |

The homeowner should understand the mechanism before they encounter the term. If they never encounter the term, they still understood the mechanism. That is a successful explanation.

**The test:** Remove every technical term from a paragraph. Does the explanation still hold? If yes, the terms are optional additions. If no, the explanation was resting on jargon rather than built on understanding.

## 4.0 Citation Discipline

### 4.1 External Citations — One Simple Rule

Nobody talks in citations. If a reader wants proof the science is real, it should be available — but it should never interrupt the reading experience.

**The rule:** Write the explanation in plain language. Put a superscript number at the end of the relevant sentence — a real link to the matching Technical Sources entry, not a decorative character. The reader who wants proof clicks through to the references at the bottom. Everyone else keeps reading. **Markup convention lives in Doc 113 §2.0** (numbered Technical Sources list with `id="src-N"`; body superscript is `<sup><a href="#src-N">N</a></sup>`) — don't restate it here, follow it.

*Right:* "Ladder falls are not an abstract risk — gutter cleaning is one of the most common reasons residential homeowners climb ladders.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>" (where Technical Sources entry 2 is the matching ladder-safety source — a working link, not the bare `[¹²]` glyph this example used to show).

**Never do this:** "According to the CDC..." / "Research shows..." / "Studies indicate..." — Source names do not belong in the prose. The explanation earns itself. The citation supports it quietly.

**When to use external citations:**
- Science or physics claims
- Safety statistics
- Warranty, approval, or manufacturer claims
- Material, fire, or code claims

**When not to use them:**
- Field-observable patterns ("pine needles accumulate on flat guards")
- Homeowner experiences ("most maintenance calls are not about leaves")
- Mechanism explanations Karen or Aaron can assert from field experience

**Quota:** 3–5 external citations per guardian cluster page, collected as numbered references at the bottom of the page. Do not over-cite.

### 4.2 Karen and Aaron — Two Different Kinds of Authority

Karen and Aaron are not interchangeable. They carry different authority for different claims.

**Karen Sager — technical authority**
Karen explains why a mechanism works, what the engineering choice is, and why the physics produces the result it does. She has the patents. She designed the product. She is the right voice when the page needs to say: "Here is why this is the correct engineering answer." Karen has field experience too — but Aaron has seen far more of it, so the field-reality voice defaults to him.

Use Karen for:
- Mechanism explanations ("here is what is actually happening at the filter opening")
- Engineering evaluation criteria ("here is what a guard must do at this failure point")
- Technical verdicts ("this is why flat installation cannot produce the same result")

**Aaron Kapfer — field authority**
Aaron explains what actually happens to homeowners and installers in the real world. He knows what people call about, what breaks in year two, what installers get wrong, and what customers see when a guard fails. He is the right voice when the page needs to say: "Here is what I have seen."

Use Aaron for:
- Symptom and problem pages ("the most common call is not about overflow")
- Maintenance expectation sections ("what homeowners discover in the second season")
- Installation pages ("what most installers skip")
- The field-reality layer in any mechanism explanation

**The principle:** Karen proves the mechanism. Aaron proves it happens. Together they give every page engineering credibility and field credibility — a combination no competitor page has.

**Bios: one author, contributor labeled (Doc 192).** A page has one **Author** (the byline and the Article schema `author`) with a bio in `## About the Author`. When the other expert is actually quoted on the page, their bio appears below it, labeled **Contributor / Field Expert** — never a second co-equal author, and never a bio for someone not cited on that page. Quoting Aaron inside the body does not, by itself, make him the author; it earns him a labeled contributor bio. Each bio carries a real, verifiable credential.

**Format for both:** One or two sentences. Plainspoken. Specific. Attribution after the statement, not before.

*Karen example:* "Moving water carries the small stuff. Still water leaves it behind." — Karen Sager
*Aaron example:* "The most frustrating calls are when the gutter is clean but the top of the guard is matted over. The homeowner bought a guard and still ended up maintaining the guard." — Aaron Kapfer

Draft expert assertions in the page. Mark for Karen's or Aaron's approval. Once approved, add to Doc 113 (Citation Resource Bank) as reusable assets.

## 5.0 Voice Variants by Page Type

The Matt Risinger quality travels to every page type. Bill Nye applies primarily to mechanism pages. Here is how the voice adjusts:

| Page Type | Matt Risinger Role | Bill Nye Role | Primary Structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Guardian Mechanism** | Expert explaining what the mechanism actually does | Full — teach physics accessibly | Five-step hierarchy (Section 2.0) |
| **Problem/Symptom** | Contractor who can read the symptom and name the cause | Minimal — explain why the symptom indicates what it does | Symptom → diagnosis → engineering requirement → solution |
| **Comparison/Category** | Builder who has installed both and picks sides | Low — explain criteria, not physics | Criteria establishment → performance against criteria → verdict |
| **Head-to-Head** | Contractor who has used both and knows the real difference | Low | What each was designed for → where each breaks down → verdict |
| **Pricing/Value** | Contractor who has given hundreds of quotes and knows what cheap means | None | What drives price → what low price means → total cost of ownership |
| **Installation** | Contractor who cleans up bad installs | Low | What proper installation involves → what goes wrong → what to ask |
| **Local/Geographic** | Regional contractor who knows the specific conditions | Low | Regional condition → specific debris or climate challenge → recommendation |
| **Guide/Pillar** | Systems thinker — the Wirecutter approach | Moderate | Category definition → evaluation criteria → how guards fail at each point → solution |
| **FAQ/Answer** | Expert giving the real answer, not the safe one | Low | Direct answer first → supporting explanation → nuance |
| **B2B/Dealer** | Business partner (not homeowner advisor) | None | Market opportunity → differentiation → dealer advantage → outcomes |

## 6.0 The Paragraph Rule

**Maximum 4 sentences per paragraph in homeowner-facing body prose.**

Short paragraphs are not a style preference — they are a comprehension requirement. Mobile readers, AI extraction systems, and homeowners who are scanning all require shorter units.

When a paragraph reaches 4 sentences and more explanation is needed, start a new paragraph.

**This is a hard fail in the auditor, not a suggestion.**

## 7.0 What AEGIS 5X Should Feel Like

AEGIS 5X should feel like the natural answer to: *"Wow, there are more failure points than I thought."*

Not a product name. Not a feature list. A discovery.

The reader should arrive at AEGIS 5X through the argument — understanding that gutter guards fail for five distinct reasons before they encounter the framework that addresses all five. The framework is the resolution, not the opening.

**The close that works:**
> "That is why AEGIS 5X exists. Gutter guards do not fail for one reason, so the answer cannot be one feature. Five failure points. Five guardians. Each one solving a specific part of the problem the others do not touch."

## 8.0 What Good Looks Like

**Good — neighbor observation:**
> "Moving water carries the small stuff. Still water leaves it behind."

**Better — with simple science:**
> "When water moves across a pitched guard, it has sideways energy. That movement carries pollen, grit, and pine needles before they can settle. When water drops straight through a flat screen, the water leaves and the debris stays."

**Complete — with technical proof offered, not required:**
> "The technical name for part of this effect is inertial impaction, but you do not need the term to understand the point: the water scrubs the opening as it turns through it."

## 9.0 What Fails This Standard

A paragraph fails this standard if:

- It leads with a technical term without a plain-language explanation first
- It leads with a source name ("According to...")
- It requires the reader to already understand engineering to follow the explanation
- It sounds like it could appear on any competitor's website without changing a word
- It explains mechanism without connecting to what the homeowner sees or pays for
- It runs longer than 4 sentences without a clear reason to continue

An article fails this standard if:

- The expert assertions (Karen/Aaron) are absent or generic
- External citations appear in more than 5 body locations
- The five-step hierarchy is not followed on mechanism sections
- Technical terms appear before their plain-language explanations
- The AEGIS 5X connection feels bolted on rather than earned by the argument

## 10.0 How Page Type Gets Identified (Workflow Architecture)

The page's structure and voice are declared at the Execution Plan level (Doc 153) before any writing or auditing begins. This is the single source of truth for which companion doc gets loaded.

This works as **two levels**, because a page answers two different questions:

- **`page_type` — architecture.** Where the page sits in the site: `pillar | cluster | local`. Drives depth, linking, word count, and writer playbook. (This is Doc 153's existing field.)
- **`page_subtype` — structure & voice.** What kind of page it is rhetorically. This is the field that selects the 316-X companion doc.

For **cluster** pages the subtype must be declared explicitly. For **pillar** pages it is `guide_pillar` (default) or `technology_architecture_pillar` (TAP). For **local** pages it is `local_geographic`.

```
page_type: cluster
page_subtype: [one of: mechanism | comparison | pricing | symptom | installation | faq | b2b]
```

## 11.0 Consistency Across Page Types

Different page types have different structures. They should still feel like they come from the same person.

**The through-line that creates consistency:**

**1. The Matt Risinger voice quality — everywhere.**
Every page, regardless of type, sounds like the trusted expert neighbor. Not a salesman. Not a legal document. Not a research paper. A person who knows this field deeply and is giving you the real answer. That quality does not change from a mechanism page to a pricing page to a FAQ page. (On B2B/MMGG pages it shifts to the business-partner register, but it is still the same trusted expert.)

**2. The evaluation close — on every page.**
Every page ends with a specific question the homeowner can ask any guard company, and a specific action if the answer is unsatisfactory ("move on," "keep looking for something that does," "that tells you something"). The question changes by page type. The pattern does not.

**3. AEGIS 5X as earned conclusion — always.**
Every page finds the natural moment to connect the topic to the failure point AEGIS 5X addresses for that subject. It is never announced at the opening. It always arrives as the logical answer to the problem the page established. The connection feels discovered, not declared.

**4. Karen and Aaron's voices — present on every page.**
Their specific claims, their field observations, their verdicts appear on every page type. A reader who reads a mechanism page, then a pricing page, then a symptom page, will encounter the same personalities giving authoritative answers in their respective domains. That consistency builds trust across the content system, not just within a single page.

**What the reader experiences:** Each page feels like a conversation with the same knowledgeable person, who happens to be answering a different question this time. The structure is different. The voice is the same.
