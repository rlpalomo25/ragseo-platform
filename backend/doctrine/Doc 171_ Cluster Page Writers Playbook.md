## 1.0 Understanding Your Role

A Cluster Page isn't a general overview or a shallow introduction to a topic. It's something more specific and more valuable than that: it's a deep, evidence-based answer to one single, evaluative question.

Think of it this way: while a pillar page sets the stage and frames the conversation, a cluster page actually answers the question a homeowner is asking when they're trying to make a decision. It's where the rubber meets the road—where comparison happens, where tradeoffs are analyzed, and where your reader gains the clarity they need to move forward confidently.

Your job as the writer is to follow the **Decision Axis** from the execution contract precisely. Every cluster page enters the Writing Tension Gradient at Stage 2 (Concern), which means you'll follow the **Concern → Consequence → Control → Confidence** arc throughout.

## 2.0 The 6-Step Writing Workflow

Here's how to build a cluster page, step by step. Follow this sequence exactly—it's been refined through experience to produce the best results.

---

### Step 1: Load the Execution Contract

Your starting point is the approved H1-H3 outline provided by your Content Manager. This is your **execution contract**—it's the blueprint you're building from.

Don't deviate from the structure, headings, or content angles defined in this document. The outline exists for a reason: it ensures every cluster page follows the same logical flow and covers what it needs to cover. Your job is to execute, not to reimagine the architecture.

---

### Step 2: Write the Body Content (Problem → Comparison → Tradeoff)

This is the heart of the article. The body is where you build credibility, provide evidence, and help your reader understand the real tradeoffs involved in their decision.

#### Reinforcing Your Core Belief

Every cluster page is built around a core belief—the central argument or tradeoff that the page is making. This belief needs to be stated or reinforced at least three times throughout the article:

1. **Early** — In the opening Problem section
2. **Mid-page** — During the Mechanism Comparison
3. **Late** — Near the Resolution or final CTA

This repetition isn't redundancy—it's reinforcement. Your reader should never have to wonder what the main point is because you're making it consistently clear.

#### The Problem Section

Start by elaborating on the specific problem defined in your H1. This isn't a generic introduction—it's a focused exploration of the particular challenge your reader is facing.

**Crucial element:** You must include a **Failure Consequence** subsection that quantifies the risk. Numbers matter here. Instead of saying "clogged gutters cause damage," say something like: *"A clogged gutter in a heavy downpour can overflow at a rate of 10 gallons per minute, saturating the fascia board and leading to rot within 2-3 seasons."* This kind of specificity builds trust and makes the problem feel real.

#### The Mechanism Comparison Section

This is where your reader gets the real value—the neutral, evidence-based comparison of how different gutter guard mechanisms actually perform against the specific problem you're addressing.

**Here's what you need to do:**

- **Restate the core tradeoff** from the parent pillar page before the comparison begins. For example: *"The fundamental tradeoff in all gutter protection is between water flow and debris filtration."* This keeps the reader grounded in the bigger picture.

- **Use standard mechanisms** as defined in the Cluster Page Type Module (Mesh Filtration, Surface Tension, etc.). Consistency matters—don't invent your own terminology.

- **Meet evidence standards.** Every claim needs support. Follow the evidence hierarchy from the Data Source Guardrails (Document 06). As a minimum, you need at least one source per mechanism and at least three sources total.

- **Include the comparison table.** The Cluster Page Type Module defines a specific table format with exact columns and evidence standards. Use it exactly as specified.

#### The Tradeoff Analysis Section

After presenting the comparison, explain the specific tradeoffs of each solution in the context of your page's decision axis. You're helping the reader understand not just *what* works, but *under what circumstances* and *for whom*.

A note on pricing: category economics (general cost ranges, value comparisons) are fair game. Brand-specific pricing is not—this isn't the place to talk about specific costs from one company.

---

### Step 3: Write the Engineered Resolution (Conditional)

This section is optional. It only gets written if the execution contract calls for it—and if it does, it's a significant moment. This is the first time a brand name (MasterShield or Klean Gutter) appears in the article.

**When you write this section:**

1. **Transition from neutral** — Start by moving from the balanced comparison to the specific, engineered solution. Make the shift feel natural, not abrupt.

2. **Connect to the tradeoff** — Explicitly state how the brand's technology solves the core tradeoff you discussed in the body. Show the reader that this isn't a brand pitch—it's a logical conclusion.

3. **Provide proof** — Back up your claims with real evidence: patent numbers, testing data, engineering diagrams. Don't ask the reader to take your word for it.

---

### Step 4: Write the Introduction & Conclusion

Once the body is solid, add the opening and closing sections. These frame everything you've built.

#### Introduction (Above-the-Fold)

Your introduction does several things:

**Key Takeaways Realization-Moment Rule — Stakes-Setter Standard**

Do not write Key Takeaways as a summary of the page. Do not write it as a cliffhanger that withholds the answer.

Write it as a stakes-setter: show the homeowner the overlooked problem, why it matters to their home, and the direction of the answer.

Each bullet should move the reader from:
"I came with a question."
to:
"I understand why this question matters more than I thought."

Favor concrete homeowner images, visible consequences, and plain-language cause-and-effect over abstract mechanism labels.

**Bullet arc (MANDATORY):** Bullets should form a directed arc — not a parallel list of stakes statements. Open with the problem the homeowner did not know to look for but will immediately recognize. Close with enough proof or direction that the full page feels worth the read, not just interesting.

**Sequencing rules for the arc:**
- **Orient before disturbing:** The opening bullet establishes something the reader already knows to be true. Bullet 2 shows how that truth is being violated. The problem needs a foundation before it can land.
- **Question before proof in the closing bullet:** When you have both an evaluation question and a proof point, the question is the climax. The proof validates the question, not the other way around.

**Reject Key Takeaways if it:**
- only previews sections;
- names proprietary mechanisms without a lived problem;
- creates curiosity without direction;
- gives technical claims without a homeowner-visible consequence.

**AEO Context:** Realization-moment Key Takeaways blocks are more AI-citable than flat summaries because they encode problem, cause, and consequence in a single compact unit — the exact format AI uses when answering questions like "why does this fail?" or "what should I know before choosing a gutter guard?" For each bullet, ask: does this answer one of those questions? If not, rewrite it.

Key Takeaways should give enough answer to build trust, but enough stakes to make the full page feel necessary.

- The first paragraph must include a **contextual link** to the parent pillar page. This maintains the hub-and-spoke structure and helps readers navigate deeper into the topic cluster.

- Address the top 2-3 FAQs from the execution contract within the first few paragraphs. Your reader should feel like their immediate questions are being answered right away.

- The CTA in this section should be a **navigational CTA**—something like *"See how all gutter guard types compare."* You're guiding the reader to explore further, not pushing them toward a transaction yet.

#### Conclusion (Logical Routing)

The final section should be titled **"Next Steps"** or **"Related Reading"**—clear, action-oriented language.

It must contain a minimum of 2-3 contextual links to adjacent cluster pages, following the routing logic defined in the Cluster Page Type Module. You're helping the reader continue their research journey logically.

---

### Step 5: Write the FAQ Section

Add the full FAQ section below your conclusion. The execution contract will specify exactly how many FAQs are required—plan for a minimum of 10, but scale up as needed to beat competitor benchmarks. More thorough FAQs signal expertise and build trust.

---

### Step 6: Add Final Components

With the main content complete, add these finishing elements:

1. **Author Bio** — Load the standard author bio from the relevant Brand System Module.

2. **Hard Conversion CTA** — Place a transactional CTA (e.g., *"Get a Free Estimate"*) at the very bottom of the page. This is where readers who are ready to act can take the next step.

3. **Title Tag & Meta Description** — Write a click-focused title tag and meta description using the formulas from the Writer's Playbook (Doc 170). This is your storefront window—make it inviting.
