## Page Purpose

The reader has a problem they can see. They searched for it. They are not researching guard types — they are diagnosing a failure that has already happened.

The page's job is to:
1. Confirm what they are seeing is real (validate the frustration)
2. Explain what the symptom reveals about the guard's design
3. Name the engineering failure that produced it
4. Position the solution as the guard that does not have that failure

**The voice is the diagnostic contractor.** Not a salesman. A contractor who has seen this symptom before and knows exactly what it means. He walks in, looks at the guard, and says: "Here's what happened and why."

---

## The Key Insight for These Pages

Symptoms are caused by design failures, not bad luck. A guard that clogs with pine needles is not a guard that got unlucky with pine needles — it is a guard that was never designed for them. A guard that develops algae in two years is not a defective guard — it is a guard whose material has no biological resistance.

The page should make this clear without blame. "You were sold a guard designed for a different problem than you have."

## Required Structure

### H1
Lead with the symptom the reader searched for. "Why Pine Needles Are Covering Your Gutter Guard (And What That Tells You About the Guard)" is stronger than a generic headline because it confirms the reader is in the right place immediately.

### H2 1 — What You Are Seeing (Validation)
Describe the symptom in detail. Make the reader feel immediately recognized — the description should be specific enough that they nod and think "yes, that is exactly what I have."

Include: what it looks like, when it happens, how long it has been building. Write from field observation — the contractor who has seen this many times.

### H2 2 — What the Symptom Reveals
This is the diagnostic step. Explain what the symptom indicates about the guard's design. Be specific: "This symptom means the guard does not have [mechanism]. Here is why."

Bill Nye applies minimally here — just enough to explain why the design produces the symptom. Not a full physics lesson.

*Example: "When pine needles accumulate on a flat guard and do not clear between rain events, that tells you one thing: the guard is not pitched steeply enough for debris to move, and the water is arriving as a droplet rather than a flowing film. There is no transport mechanism. Debris lands and stays."*

### H2 3 — What Would Have Prevented It
Name the specific engineering characteristic that this guard lacks. Explain what a guard with that characteristic does differently and why it produces a different outcome.

This is where AEGIS 5X enters — naturally, as the engineering answer to the specific failure the reader is experiencing. Do not force it. If the symptom is surface clogging, SelfClean Mesh is the relevant guardian. If the symptom is overflow in heavy rain, HydroVortex is the answer.

### H2 4 — What to Do Now
Give the reader practical next steps:
1. How to assess whether their current guard is recoverable or should be replaced
2. What to look for in a replacement (the evaluation criteria specific to their symptom)
3. What to ask a guard company before replacing

### H2 5 — The Evaluation Close
3–4 specific questions the reader should ask any guard company before they replace. The questions should be ones that only a correctly engineered guard can answer satisfactorily.

## Voice Rules for Symptom Pages

**Do:** Validate the symptom before diagnosing it. The reader is frustrated. Acknowledge that first.
**Do not:** Open with "Most homeowners experience..." — that is generic. Open with the specific symptom they searched for.

**Do:** Explain what the symptom reveals about the design. This is what the reader actually wants to understand.
**Do not:** Jump to the product pitch before the diagnosis is complete.

**Do:** Be specific about what "fixed" looks like. What will they see after the problem is solved?
**Do not:** Promise elimination of problems in every condition. The maintenance frequency section rules from guardian cluster pages apply here too.

---

## Buyer State Note

Symptom pages serve **replacement buyers** — homeowners who already own a guard and are experiencing a failure. They are more frustrated and more skeptical than first-time buyers. Earning their trust requires:

- Acknowledging their experience before explaining the mechanism
- Not dismissing what they were sold (even if it was the wrong product)
- Being specific about what went wrong — the design failure, not user error

A replacement buyer who feels understood by a page is far more likely to convert than one who feels lectured to.

---

## Citation Rules for Symptom Pages

External proof: Minimal. Field observation carries these pages. Use external proof only if:
- A specific material claim needs support (copper's biological resistance)
- A safety claim is made (ladder risk from repeated maintenance)
- A scientific mechanism is named technically

Internal expert assertions: 2–3 required. At least one should come from Aaron Kapfer — he is the field-experience voice for what guards fail at in practice.

*Example: "The most frustrating calls are when the gutter is clean, but the top of the guard is matted over. The homeowner bought a guard and still ended up maintaining the guard." — Aaron Kapfer*
