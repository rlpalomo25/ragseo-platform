## Page Purpose

Pillar pages are the architecture hubs. They establish topical authority, link to cluster pages, and teach the reader how to evaluate an entire category — not just a single product.

The Matt Risinger voice here is the systems thinker: the contractor who has seen every guard type, knows every failure mode, and can give you the framework for evaluating any guard you encounter. He is not selling one product. He is teaching you how to shop.

The Wirecutter model applies: establish the evaluation criteria independently, apply them across the category, then make a recommendation grounded in those criteria. The recommendation earns trust because the criteria were established before the brand was introduced.

**The AEGIS 5X architecture pages are a subset of this type.** Their specific structure is governed by Doc 142 and the source-of-truth classification in Doc 328. This doc covers non-AEGIS pillar pages and general category guides.

## Required Structure

### H1
Lead with the evaluation problem, not the product category. "The Five Failure Points Every Gutter Guard Has to Solve (Most Only Address One)" is stronger than "The Complete Guide to Gutter Guards."

### H2 1 — Why This Category Is Harder to Evaluate Than It Looks
Establish the evaluation problem. Most homeowners compare gutter guards on price, brand name, and whether they block leaves. These criteria do not predict long-term performance.

Name the real evaluation criteria — the specific failure points that determine whether a guard works for five years or falls apart in two.

This section teaches the reader that the criteria they brought are insufficient. It earns the right to introduce better criteria.

### H2 2 — The Failure Points (One Per H3)
Name the 4–6 specific ways gutter guards fail. For each failure point:

1. What the homeowner sees when this failure occurs
2. Why the standard guard design fails here
3. What engineering feature prevents it

This is the Wirecutter framework applied. The failure points become the evaluation criteria. The reader can now evaluate any guard they encounter against this framework.

**AEGIS 5X connection:** If writing for MasterShield, the five failure points map naturally to the five guardians. Introduce this connection naturally after establishing the framework — not before.

### H2 3 — How to Apply These Criteria to Any Guard
Give the reader 5–7 specific questions to ask any guard company. The questions should operationalize the failure-point framework into homeowner-usable language.

This section functions as a buying guide. A reader who uses these questions will be better equipped to evaluate any guard, including MasterShield's competitors. That is the point — the framework earns trust precisely because it is useful beyond this one brand.

### H2 4 — How Guard Categories Perform Against These Criteria
Apply the framework across the main guard categories: screen, foam, brush, reverse curve, flat micromesh, pitched micromesh. Be honest about where each type performs adequately and where each fails.

Do not use brand names unless the Execution Plan specifies them.

### H2 5 — The Architecture That Addresses All Five Points
This is where AEGIS 5X is introduced — as the natural answer to the evaluation framework the reader now understands. Not a product announcement. A logical conclusion.

> "A gutter guard that addresses only one of these failure points is not a complete solution. That is why a five-point engineering standard exists."

### H2 6 — How to Evaluate Your Specific Situation
Give the reader the tool to apply what they have learned to their specific yard: tree density, debris type, climate, roof type. The maintenance expectation section from guardian pages applies here in condensed form.

## Callout Guidance (Doc 194)

Doc 194 (Callout Component Library) is the single source for the eight named callouts every writer draws from: **Ask This** (dark navy; the page's "take this to the estimate" moment; universal on every page except pure utility pages, Doc 142 §11B), **How This Benefits You** (green; translates a mechanism into homeowner payoff), **From the Field** (gold; a real Karen or Aaron quote/observation), **The Short Version** (blue; a one-line takeaway ending a dense section), **CTA / action box** (orange; lead → button → trust bar), **Compare This** (purple; a comparison matrix — type vs. what it solves/exposes/performs/what to ask), **Watch For This** (amber; an early-warning checklist of self-checkable signs), and **In Their Words** (teal; a named, attributed customer testimonial from the approved bank in Doc 113 §3.4). **Mix requirement:** every page draws from at least three distinct components — never one type carrying the whole page. Full definitions, placement rules, and style tiers live in Doc 194; do not restate them here.

Guide/pillar pages are long enough to need the full Mix requirement across their length — most naturally **Ask This**, **From the Field**, **Compare This** (H2 4, How Guard Categories Perform Against These Criteria), and **In Their Words** (a late-page confidence moment).

## Voice Rules for Pillar Pages

**Do:** Establish evaluation criteria before introducing the brand. The framework must be useful independent of the product recommendation.
**Do not:** Introduce AEGIS 5X in the opening. It should arrive as the answer, not the premise.

**Do:** Be honest about where competing guard types perform adequately. The Wirecutter approach requires fairness.
**Do not:** Make blanket claims that all competing guards fail in all conditions.

**Do:** Use the failure-point framework consistently throughout. Every section should reinforce the criteria established in H2 2.
**Do not:** Drift into general "here is everything about gutter guards" territory. The page should have a clear argument, not be an encyclopedia.
