# **TIER 1: FAILURES (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

  ## **1\. Do gutter guards actually work?**

\[propagation\]

hook: "If debris sheds on impact, buildup never has a starting point."

related\_truth: \["6", "7", "11"\] | related\_edge: \["EC-04"\]

\[/propagation\]

Some do. A lot don't. The difference shows up when debris is allowed to stay.

Most gutter guards are flat because engineering a system that installs at the actual angle of your roof is a difficult mechanical problem that most manufacturers haven't solved. The problem is that **a flat gutter guard on a pitched roof is a shelf**, and things always pile up on shelves—debris, ice, and organic material.

Once debris sits, it bonds as it dries and changes how the next rainfall behaves. \[PRODUCT\] uses \[SYSTEM\] to align with the roofline so debris hits the surface and continues off the edge instead of settling.

**If nothing sits, nothing builds up.**

## **2\. Do gutter guards clog?**

\[propagation\]

hook: "A clog is not a filter problem. It's a debris-staying problem."

related\_truth: \["6", "7", "11", "12"\] | related\_edge: \["EC-04"\]

\[/propagation\]

Yes, many do. They don't fail right away; they degrade over months and years.

Clogging usually starts on top of the guard when a flat installation creates a shelf for debris to settle. Through repeated wet-dry cycles, this material bonds into a solid mat that blocks water before it reaches the trough.

A clog is not a filter problem. It's a debris-staying problem.

\[SYSTEM\] is designed so debris never stays long enough to bond. Now that you know how \[SYSTEM\] prevents stabilization, ask any other guard how their design prevents debris from turning into a bonded mat.

## **4\. Do gutter guards work in heavy rain?**

\[propagation\]

hook: "If water keeps moving across the surface, volume stops being the breaking point."

related\_truth: \["1", "5", "12"\] | related\_edge: \["EC-02"\]

\[/propagation\]

Heavy rain is where most systems get exposed.

Flat filter guards rely on gravity alone to pull water through, creating a "puddle dynamic" where water must pool before it drains. If water has to enter through a narrow edge opening, the volume during a downpour exceeds that capacity and overshoots.

\[SYSTEM\] captures water across the surface at the angle it was already traveling, controlling the flow before velocity exceeds intake stability.

**If water keeps moving across the surface, volume stops being the bottleneck.**

## **5. Why do plastic or vinyl gutter guards fail faster than metal ones?**

[propagation]

hook: "The roof itself chemically destroys them. Asphalt shingle oils pull the plasticizers right out of the vinyl."

related_truth: ["chemistry_2"] | related_edge: ["EC-31"]

[/propagation]

Most people assume plastic or vinyl gutter guards fail because they are cheap or because the sun bakes them. UV damage is real, but the primary cause of rapid failure is a destructive chemical reaction with the roof itself.

Asphalt shingles are petroleum-based. When they get hot, they leach microscopic oils down the roof. When those oils contact flexible PVC or vinyl gutter guards, they act like a chemical sponge, pulling the softening agents (plasticizers) completely out of the vinyl. Without those plasticizers, the vinyl undergoes an irreversible change: it embrittles, shrinks, warps, and can shatter like glass in the cold.

Even uPVC (unplasticized PVC) is highly vulnerable to thermal expansion and UV degradation at the roof edge. When you force a plastic product under heavy asphalt shingles, you are placing it in the exact environment that will chemically and thermally destroy it.

### **How does a gutter guard actually clean itself?**

[propagation]

hook: "Moving water carries the small stuff. Still water leaves it behind."

related_truth: ["physics_14", "physics_15", "physics_18", "debris_4"]

[/propagation]

It has to be steep, and then it does three things. First the guard has to sit at a real pitch, steep enough that debris slides instead of resting. Then moving water carries debris off the surface before it can settle, the openings get rinsed clean every time it rains, and that same flow keeps an oily film from building up and turning the surface into glue. A flat guard cannot do any of it, because flat installation gives up the moving water that makes all three work.

**If the water keeps moving, debris never gets to stay.**

### **Why does pitch matter so much for a self-cleaning guard?**

[propagation]

hook: "The angle has to come first. A premium mesh laid flat is still a flat surface."

related_truth: ["physics_14", "debris_4"]

[/propagation]

Nothing moves across a flat surface. At your roof's angle, debris slides toward the edge and water keeps moving across the guard instead of dropping straight through. Lay that same mesh flat and gravity pulls straight down into the debris, the water arrives as a falling drop with no sideways push, and everything just sits. That is why pitch is the prerequisite, not a feature.

**The angle sheds debris; the flat shelf collects it.**

### **Does heavy rain clog a gutter guard, or clean it?**

[propagation]

hook: "On the right guard, the harder it rains, the harder it cleans."

related_truth: ["physics_15"] | related_edge: ["EC-02"]

[/propagation]

On a flat guard, a downpour packs debris into the openings: more water, more pressure, a tighter clog. On a guard at roof pitch, the same storm does the opposite. The moving water clears the openings, and it clears them more aggressively the harder it comes down. Same weather, opposite result, and it comes down entirely to whether the water is moving across the surface or just pressing down on it.

**Heavy rain is where weak designs show themselves.**

### **Why do gutter guards get worse after a year or two, even when nothing looks wrong?**

[propagation]

hook: "The guard didn't change. What the debris lands on did."

related_truth: ["physics_18", "debris_4"]

[/propagation]

There is a kind of grime that does not act like debris. Roof oils, dust, and fine silt do not land as pieces; they settle as a film that coats the surface, and that film slowly turns the guard into something sticky that holds everything landing afterward. Most guards have no defense against it, which is why one that worked the first year collects faster by the third. A guard at pitch rinses that film off with every rain, so the surface never turns against it.

**A gutter guard can look clean and still be losing the surface that makes it work.**

* **Hidden-State Rule:** Surface appearance does not always reflect hydraulic behavior; check for underside growth.
